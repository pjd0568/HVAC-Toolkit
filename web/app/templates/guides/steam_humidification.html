Steam humidification is usually drawn as a vertical line on the psychrometric chart — constant dry-bulb, rising moisture — because the temperature change during injection is small. Thermodynamically it is not a zero-enthalpy process: steam adds both latent and sensible energy to the airstream, so moist-air enthalpy rises. This article sets out the mass and energy balances, explains why the dry-bulb drift is typically only about a degree, and compares the ideal isothermal model used in most design tools with the rigorous enthalpy-slope solution.

1. Mass and energy balance

Dry steam enters the duct already as vapour — there is no phase change in the airstream itself. The energy added is therefore set by the steam mass flow and the specific enthalpy of the steam at the injection condition.

Dry-air mass is unchanged across the humidifier:

ṁa1 = ṁa2 = ṁa

Water-vapour mass rises by the injected steam:

ṁa·W1 + ṁw = ṁa·W2   ⇒   ṁw = ṁa·(W2 − W1)

Energy balance on the moist-air stream:

ṁa·h1 + ṁw·hg = ṁa·h2

Substituting the moisture balance gives the governing state equation:

h2 = h1 + (W2 − W1)·hg
  • ṁa — dry-air mass flow (kg/s)
  • ṁw — injected steam mass flow (kg/s)
  • W1, W2 — entering / leaving humidity ratio (kgw/kga)
  • h1, h2 — moist-air enthalpy (kJ/kga)
  • hg — specific enthalpy of the injected steam (kJ/kgw), set by supply pressure / temperature
a, W₁, h₁ State 2 ṁa, W₂, h₂ Steam ṁw, hg already vapour — no phase change in the duct
Energy crossing the humidifier boundary: dry-air flow is constant; steam mass and steam enthalpy raise both moisture and moist-air enthalpy.

2. Why dry-bulb barely moves

Moist-air enthalpy is written (ASHRAE Fundamentals form):

h = cpa·Tdb + W·(hfg,0 + cpw·Tdb)
  • cpa ≈ 1.006 kJ/kg·K (0.240 Btu/lb·°F) — dry air
  • cpw ≈ 1.86 kJ/kg·K (0.444 Btu/lb·°F) — water vapour
  • hfg,0 ≈ 2501 kJ/kg (1061 Btu/lb) — latent heat of vaporization at 0 °C

Steam at Tsteam (for example 100 °C / 212 °F) mixing into air at Tdb1 (for example 21 °C / 70 °F) contributes two parts:

  1. Latent (~98–99%). The phase-change energy already paid by the boiler or electrode humidifier (~2257 kJ/kg / 970 Btu/lb at 100 °C) enters the airstream as an increase in latent content.
  2. Sensible (~1–2%). The vapour is hotter than the room air, so a small dry-bulb rise appears:
ΔTdb ≈ [ṁw·cpw·(Tsteam − Tdb1)] / [ṁa·(cpa + W2·cpw)]

Quantitative example. Comfort humidification from 20% to 45% RH at 21 °C (70 °F):

  • ΔW ≈ +0.0039 kgw/kga
  • hg at 100 °C ≈ 2676 kJ/kg
  • Calculated dry-bulb drift ≈ +1.1 to +1.5 °C (+2.0 to +2.7 °F)

That is why charts and sizing tools treat the process as isothermal: the slope is steep, almost vertical, and the temperature error is usually smaller than sensor or load uncertainty.

3. Steam vs adiabatic humidification

Steam injection and evaporative (atomising / pad) humidification leave opposite signatures on the chart:

Parameter Steam injection Atomised / evaporative (adiabatic)
Enthalpy change (Δh) Increases — h2 > h1 Nearly constant — h2 ≈ h1
Dry-bulb temperature Virtually constant (ΔTdb ≈ +1 °C) Falls (ΔTdb ≪ 0)
Wet-bulb temperature Increases Constant
External energy High — boiler or electric steam None at the humidifier — absorbs room sensible heat
Process path Near-vertical (+W at nearly fixed Tdb) Along the constant wet-bulb line
From the same entering state, steam climbs almost vertically (moisture up, dry-bulb nearly fixed, enthalpy up). Adiabatic humidification follows the wet-bulb line toward cooler, wetter air at nearly constant enthalpy.

4. Modelling guidelines for calculation engines

Two implementations are common; choose by the accuracy the job needs.

Approach A — Ideal isothermal model

  • Assumption: Tdb2 = Tdb1
  • State 2: Tdb2 = Tdb1, W2 = W1 + ṁw/ṁa
  • Use for: standard load calculations, AHU sizing, and chart plots

This is the model used by the Heating & Humidification and AHU tools in this app — the chart path is vertical and the steam duty is the isothermal enthalpy rise.

Approach B — Rigorous energy balance (enthalpy slope)

  • Specify steam enthalpy hg from supply-line pressure / temperature
  • W2 = W1 + ṁw/ṁa
  • h2 = h1 + (W2 − W1)·hg
  • Solve Tdb2 = f(h2, W2) from the moist-air enthalpy equation (closed form or iteration)
  • Use for: cleanrooms, laboratories, and energy audits where the ~1 °C drift matters
