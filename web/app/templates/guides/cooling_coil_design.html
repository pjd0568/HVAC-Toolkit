A cooling coil looks like the simplest component in an air handling unit — a block of finned tube with water or refrigerant on one side and air on the other — but it carries most of the plant's energy and most of the risk of a bad selection: nuisance condensate, frosting, or a unit that cools fine but never controls humidity. This guide covers what the coil is physically doing to the air, why some coils dehumidify and others don't, and the handful of selection decisions that follow from the psychrometrics. Two kinds of heat, one coil

Every cooling coil removes heat in two distinct forms, and telling them apart is the first step in understanding a selection. Sensible heat shows up as a temperature drop — it's what a thermometer in the airstream registers. Latent heat is the energy tied up in water vapour, released the instant that vapour condenses on the coil's fin surface; the dry-bulb temperature barely moves while a substantial amount of energy leaves the airstream. The coil's total duty is simply the enthalpy the air gives up:

Q_total = m × (h_on − h_off)

with m the dry-air mass flow rate in kg/s and h in kJ/kg. The sensible portion alone follows the familiar specific-heat relationship:

Q_sensible = m × 1.005 × (T_on − T_off)

and whatever remains of the total is latent. The sensible heat ratio, Q_sensible ÷ Q_total, is a useful shorthand for what kind of duty a coil is doing: a typical comfort-cooling office coil runs at an SHR around 0.65–0.8, while a coil dedicated to drying already-humid air (a pool hall, a process area) can sit well below that.

Why some coils dehumidify and others just cool

Whether a coil removes moisture at all comes down to one comparison: the coil's apparatus dew point (ADP) — in effect, the mean temperature of the coil's wetted surface — against the dew point of the air arriving at the coil. If the ADP sits above the entering air's dew point, nowhere on the coil is cold enough to condense water and the entire duty is sensible; the coil runs perfectly dry. Once the ADP drops below the entering dew point, the coldest parts of the fin pack start condensing moisture even while most of the airstream is still unsaturated, and the duty splits into sensible and latent.

This is exactly why the water or refrigerant temperature is a design decision, not just a plant-room setpoint. A conventional 6°C flow / 12°C return chilled water circuit pushes the ADP low enough to dehumidify seriously. A high-temperature circuit run at, say, 14°C flow / 17°C return — as used for passive chilled beams and some fan coil systems — deliberately keeps the ADP above room dew point specifically so the coil stays dry and there is no condensate to manage at the terminal unit.

The bypass factor — why the process isn't one clean line

Not every parcel of air that crosses the coil actually touches the fin surface. The fraction that does is the coil's contact factor (CF); the fraction that streams through untouched, still at close to the entering condition, is the bypass factor (BF = 1 − CF). Idealised textbook treatments draw the process as a single straight line from the air-on point to the ADP — a coil with CF = 1, contacting all of the air. Real coils fall short of that, and the off-coil condition sits back along that line toward air-on, in proportion to the bypass fraction:

T_off = ADP + BF × (T_on − ADP)
The off-coil point sits back along the air-on/ADP line by the bypass fraction — a shallow-fin, high-face-velocity coil bypasses more air and lands closer to air-on; a deep, tightly-finned coil at low face velocity pulls the off-coil point down close to the ADP itself.

Because enthalpy and moisture content are properties of the state, not the path taken to reach it, the kW figures a calculator reports depend only on the air-on and off-coil end points — whichever curve the real coil traces between them, the duty is exact for those two states. Contact factor itself rises with coil depth (more rows) and fin density, and falls as face velocity increases; predicting it precisely needs the manufacturer's construction data (rows, fin spacing, circuiting), which is why final coil selection belongs in selection software — the psychrometrics tell you what duty to ask of it, not which exact coil to buy.

Inside the coil block

A wet coil in cross-section: rows of finned tube in the airflow direction, condensate forming on the fin surface and draining to a pan below the full coil face. Face velocity, row count and fin density (below) are set by what this physical picture needs to do — condense enough water without stripping it back into the airstream.

Selection decisions that follow from the psychrometrics

Face velocity. Aim for roughly 2.0–2.5 m/s across the finned face. Push much past about 2.5–2.8 m/s on a wet coil and condensate starts stripping off the fins into the ductwork downstream — moisture carryover — so either fit eliminators after the coil or grow the face area. A slower face velocity also buys contact factor and cuts air-side pressure drop, at the cost of a physically bigger coil and casing.

Rows and fin density. More rows and tighter fin spacing raise both contact factor and latent capacity, but they raise air-side pressure drop too, and a tightly finned wet coil is harder to keep clean. Hygiene-sensitive applications (food production, healthcare) often trade fin density down and coil depth up rather than running a hard-to-clean, closely finned block.

Flow temperature and ΔT. The flow temperature is what sets the achievable ADP; the flow/return ΔT sets the water flow rate and, with it, pump energy. Widening the ΔT (6°C/14°C rather than 6°C/12°C) trims pumping energy but narrows the surface-temperature margin — worth rechecking the coil still meets the latent duty at the wider ΔT. On DX plant the evaporating temperature plays the same role as ADP directly, with suction pressure control setting how low it can go.

Condensate. A dehumidifying coil sheds real quantities of water at full load (the moisture-removed figure a calculator reports is the number to design the drain around). That means a corrosion-resistant drain pan spanning the full coil face, a trap deep enough to overcome the fan static pressure — draw-through and blow-through put the pan on opposite sides of atmospheric, which flips which way the trap has to work — and a visible tundish for commissioning checks. A blocked or under-sized trap is one of the most common causes of AHU water damage.

Frost. Cooling to saturation has a hard floor. Once the duty needs off-coil air down around 3–5°C, the evaporating or flow temperature is at or close to 0°C and the coil enters frosting territory: condensate ices on the fins, airflow chokes, and capacity falls away. Below roughly 0°C off-coil, a conventional wet coil can't meet the duty at all — the options are a glycol circuit with frost protection logic, DX with a managed defrost cycle, or, for genuinely low dew points, desiccant dehumidification instead of a cooling coil.

Reheat. Whenever the required supply condition sits below the saturation curve at its target moisture content, the coil has to overcool past the supply dry-bulb to reach the right moisture level, and something downstream has to warm it back up — both the cooling and the reheat load are real energy, and a calculator covering the process should report both. Before defaulting to new-energy LPHW or electric reheat, check what's already available: a heat-pipe or run-around coil wrapped around the cooling coil, or hot-gas/condenser reheat on DX plant, both recover heat that would otherwise be rejected and reduce the coil's own duty at the same time. Many current energy codes restrict new-energy reheat outright, which makes this less of an optional efficiency measure than it used to be.

Margins and part-load behaviour

Fouling, fin damage and day-to-day variation in wetted-surface performance all erode a coil's rated capacity, so it's normal practice to carry around 10% margin on the calculated duty when sizing the chiller or condensing unit behind it. Part load deserves a separate look: a coil sized generously for a peak latent duty can, once a modulating valve throttles flow at part load, run a warmer mean surface and effectively stop dehumidifying — space temperature holds steady while relative humidity quietly drifts up. Where humidity control genuinely matters, face-and-bypass control, a DX hot-gas reheat wrap-around circuit, or splitting cooling and dedicated dehumidification across separate plant are all more robust than relying on a single modulating cooling valve.
